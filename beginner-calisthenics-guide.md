# Your First 4 Weeks of Calisthenics

A simple, three-days-per-week plan for adults who are new to exercise

## What calisthenics is

Calisthenics is strength training that uses your body weight as resistance. A squat asks your legs to move your body; a push-up asks your chest, shoulders, and arms to push it; a row or pull-up asks your back and arms to pull it.

You do not need a gym or advanced skills. Bodyweight and home-based resistance training can improve strength and physical function, and a simple routine you can repeat consistently matters more than a complicated “perfect” program.[1]

This guide builds six basic movement patterns:

- Squat: bend and straighten the knees and hips.
- Push: move your body away from a surface.
- Pull: draw your body or resistance toward you.
- Bridge: extend, or straighten, the hips.
- Brace: hold the trunk steady.
- Hang and pull: prepare for pull-ups with controlled assistance.

The wider health target for adults includes strengthening all major muscle groups on at least two days each week. Strength exercise complements rather than replaces aerobic activity such as brisk walking.[3]

## Before you begin

This is general fitness guidance, not diagnosis or treatment.

Get advice from a qualified healthcare or exercise professional before starting if you have an injury, health condition, unexplained symptoms, recent surgery or other recent health event, are pregnant or recently gave birth, or are unsure whether this plan suits you.[2] A qualified physiotherapist or appropriately certified trainer can also adapt movements to your needs.

During exercise:

- Stop immediately if you feel pain or become unwell. Seek appropriate medical advice if symptoms concern you or do not settle.[2]
- Treat sudden chest pain or pressure, fainting, or severe/unusual shortness of breath as urgent symptoms: stop exercising and seek medical help.[4]
- Expect muscles to work, warm up, and tire. Do not push through sharp, electrical, or worsening pain.
- Use a smooth, controlled pace. Do not bounce or drop into positions.
- Keep breathing. Exhale through the hardest part; do not deliberately hold your breath.
- Finish each set while you could still perform about 2 good repetitions. Technique should not fall apart.
- Check the floor and equipment before every session. Use a clear, nonslip space.

## What you need

Required:

- A clear patch of floor
- A stable wall
- A truly stable chair, counter, or railing that will not slide or tip
- Comfortable clothes and supportive footwear, or bare feet on a safe nonslip surface
- A timer or phone stopwatch

For pulling work, choose one safe option:

- A resistance band in good condition, used exactly as its manufacturer directs
- A suspension trainer attached to a manufacturer-approved, weight-rated anchor
- A sturdy fixed horizontal bar installed and rated for exercise
- A gym cable or row machine

Never use a loose table edge, lightweight chair, door handle, towel trapped in a door, tree branch, or unverified fixture as an anchor. Check bands for cracks, thinning, or tears and keep your face out of their recoil path.

Helpful but optional: an exercise mat, a notebook, and a low step.

## The effort rule

Choose the easiest version that lets you complete every repetition with control while keeping about 2 repetitions “in reserve.” Repetitions in reserve means how many more good repetitions you think you could perform before technique fails.

- Too hard: you cannot reach the bottom of the range, hold your position, or keep smooth technique. Switch to the easier alternative now.
- About right: the last 2 repetitions feel challenging, but still look like the first.
- Too easy: you finish the top of the range for every set and could do 4 or more extra repetitions. Use the progression rule after the session.

You do not need to train until you cannot do another repetition; advanced methods such as training to failure are optional for the average healthy adult.[1]

## Five-minute warm-up

Warming up means gradually preparing your joints, muscles, and breathing for the session.

1. March in place — 60 seconds, easy pace.
2. Shoulder rolls and arm circles — 5 each direction.
3. Hip hinges — 8 repetitions. Soften your knees, push your hips back, then stand tall.
4. Sit-to-stands from a chair — 8 easy repetitions.
5. Wall push-ups — 6 easy repetitions.
6. Practice one easy set of the first two workout movements — 5 repetitions each.

The warm-up should make you feel ready, not tired. Add a few minutes if you are still stiff or cold.

## Movement guide

### 1. Squat

Setup: Stand with feet about hip- to shoulder-width apart, toes pointed slightly outward if comfortable. Keep your whole foot on the floor.

Do it:

1. Brace your middle gently, as if preparing for a cough.
2. Send hips slightly back while bending knees.
3. Let knees track in the same direction as your toes.
4. Lower only as far as you can keep your heels down and torso controlled.
5. Push the floor away and stand tall. Do not force the knees backward at the top.

Easier: Sit-to-stand from a firm chair; use your hands lightly on a counter if needed.

Harder later: Lower to a chair without sitting, use a lower target, pause for 1 second near the bottom, or wear a lightly loaded backpack.

Common fix: If heels lift or knees cave inward, shorten the depth and slow down.

### 2. Incline push-up or knee push-up

Incline push-up setup: Place hands slightly wider than shoulders on a wall, counter, or sturdy railing. Walk feet back until your body forms a straight line from head to heels.

Do it:

1. Tighten abdomen and buttocks gently; keep head in line with the body.
2. Bend elbows about 30–60 degrees away from your sides—not straight out like a T.
3. Lower your chest toward the support while your body moves as one unit.
4. Press the support away until arms are straight but not forced backward.

Easier: Use a more upright surface, such as a wall.

Harder: Use a lower stable surface. When floor work becomes appropriate, use a knee push-up: knees down, body in a straight line from knees through hips to head. Do not hinge at the hips.

Common fix: If hips sag or shoulders shrug, use a higher incline.

### 3. Row

Preferred beginner version — seated resistance-band row:

1. Sit tall with legs comfortably extended and knees slightly bent.
2. Loop the middle of an intact band around the soles of both feet; keep it low and centered. Hold one end in each hand. Do not use this setup if the band can slip from your footwear.
3. Start with arms long and shoulders relaxed.
4. Pull elbows back toward your ribs, bringing hands toward the lower ribs.
5. Pause briefly; return slowly until arms are long again. Do not round or jerk the torso.

Alternative: Use a gym row machine or a suspension-trainer row with a rated anchor. For suspension rows, keep the body straight and make the movement easier by standing more upright.

Easier: Use a lighter band, shorten the range, or stand more upright.

Harder: Use a slightly stronger band, pause for 1–2 seconds with elbows back, or make a suspension row more horizontal.

Common fix: Keep shoulders away from ears and resist leaning backward to create momentum.

### 4. Glute bridge

Setup: Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and about hip-width. Bring heels close enough that fingertips nearly reach them.

Do it:

1. Gently brace the abdomen.
2. Press through the feet and squeeze the buttocks to lift hips.
3. Stop when shoulders, hips, and knees form a comfortable line. Do not arch the lower back to lift higher.
4. Pause briefly, then lower slowly.

Easier: Lift through a smaller comfortable range.

Harder later: Add a 2-second pause at the top or use a staggered stance, with one foot slightly ahead of the other.

Common fix: If the lower back feels pinched, reduce height and think “ribs down, squeeze the buttocks.”

### 5. Plank

Incline plank setup: Put forearms or hands on a stable wall or counter and step back. Stack shoulders above elbows or hands.

Do it:

1. Make a straight line from head to heels.
2. Tighten abdomen and buttocks gently while breathing normally.
3. Stop the hold when hips sag, hips rise, breath-holding starts, or you cannot keep the neck relaxed.

Easier: Use a wall or a higher surface; hold for less time.

Harder: Use a lower surface, then progress to a knee plank on the floor. Only after that, try a full forearm plank on toes.

Common fix: A shorter, clean hold is better than a longer sagging hold.

### 6. Assisted pull-up progression

Use a purpose-built pull-up bar or gym station that is secure and rated for your body weight. Do not jump to an untested bar. Start at Step 1; move up only when the stated work is controlled and symptom-free.

Step 1 — Band pulldown: Attach a band overhead only to a manufacturer-approved anchor. Kneel or sit, start with arms overhead, and pull elbows toward your ribs. Easier option: use a lighter band or a gym lat-pulldown machine with light resistance.

Step 2 — Supported hang: On a low bar, keep feet on a box or floor so the legs carry much of your weight. Hold with shoulders comfortable, not forced. Start with 5–10 seconds. Skip this if hanging bothers your shoulders.

Step 3 — Scapular pull: From the same foot-supported position and with straight elbows, gently draw shoulders down away from ears, lifting the body slightly; relax with control. The movement is small.

Step 4 — Foot-assisted pull-up: Keep one or both feet on a box. Use as much leg help as needed to pull until the upper chest approaches the bar; lower for 2–3 seconds.

Step 5 — Full pull-up, later: Attempt only after you can perform 3 sets of 5 controlled foot-assisted repetitions with minimal leg help. There is no deadline; many beginners need longer than four weeks.

If you do not have a verified overhead setup, keep doing rows and band pulldowns rather than improvising an anchor.

## How to run each workout

Train on three nonconsecutive days—for example Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Each session should take roughly 25–40 minutes.

1. Complete the warm-up.
2. Perform one set of an exercise, rest for the listed time, then repeat until all sets are done.
3. Move to the next exercise.
4. Use the effort rule throughout.
5. Complete the cooldown.

A repetition (rep) is one complete movement. A set is a group of repetitions. A hold is time spent keeping one position. Rest means easy breathing and no strength work.

## Four-week starter program

Use one variation of each exercise that fits your current level. “Each side” applies only if you choose an uneven variation; beginners can use the standard two-sided versions.

### Week 1 — Learn the movements

Complete all three sessions:

| Exercise | Work | Rest | Easier alternative |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Chair sit-to-stand or squat | 2 × 6–8 reps | 60–90 sec | Higher chair; light hand support |
| Wall or incline push-up | 2 × 5–8 reps | 60–90 sec | More upright wall push-up |
| Seated band or supported row | 2 × 6–8 reps | 60–90 sec | Lighter band; shorter range |
| Glute bridge | 2 × 8 reps | 60 sec | Smaller lift |
| Incline plank | 2 × 10–15 sec | 45–60 sec | Higher surface; shorter hold |
| Band pulldown or supported hang | 2 × 6–8 reps OR 5–10 sec | 60–90 sec | Lighter pulldown; more foot support |

Goal: Finish feeling as though you could have done a little more. Technique comes first.

### Week 2 — Add a little practice

Complete all three sessions:

| Exercise | Work | Rest | Easier alternative |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Chair sit-to-stand or squat | 2 × 8–10 reps | 60–90 sec | Week 1 version |
| Incline or knee push-up | 2 × 6–10 reps | 60–90 sec | Higher incline |
| Seated band or supported row | 2 × 8–10 reps | 60–90 sec | Lighter band; more upright |
| Glute bridge | 2 × 10–12 reps | 60 sec | Smaller lift |
| Incline plank | 2 × 15–20 sec | 45–60 sec | Higher surface; shorter hold |
| Pulldown, supported hang, or scapular pull | 2 × 8–10 reps OR 8–15 sec | 60–90 sec | Return one step |

Goal: Add repetitions or seconds only while form remains steady.

### Week 3 — Add one set

Complete all three sessions:

| Exercise | Work | Rest | Easier alternative |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Squat variation | 3 × 8–10 reps | 60–90 sec | Chair sit-to-stand |
| Push-up variation | 3 × 6–10 reps | 60–90 sec | Higher incline |
| Row variation | 3 × 8–10 reps | 60–90 sec | Lighter band; more upright |
| Glute bridge | 3 × 10–12 reps | 60 sec | 2 sets or smaller lift |
| Plank variation | 3 × 15–25 sec | 45–60 sec | Higher surface; shorter hold |
| Pulldown, supported hang, or scapular pull | 3 × 6–10 reps OR 10–20 sec | 60–90 sec | 2 sets or return one step |

Goal: Keep the same exercise versions while adapting to the extra set. If Week 2 was difficult, repeat Week 2 instead.

### Week 4 — Build control, not speed

Complete all three sessions:

| Exercise | Work | Rest | Easier alternative |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Squat variation | 3 × 10–12 reps | 60–90 sec | Chair sit-to-stand; fewer reps |
| Push-up variation | 3 × 8–12 reps | 60–90 sec | Higher incline; fewer reps |
| Row variation | 3 × 10–12 reps | 60–90 sec | Lighter band; more upright |
| Glute bridge | 3 × 12–15 reps | 60 sec | Smaller lift; fewer reps |
| Plank variation | 3 × 20–30 sec | 45–60 sec | Higher surface; shorter hold |
| Pulldown, supported hang, scapular pull, or foot-assisted pull-up | 3 × 8–12 reps OR 15–25 sec | 60–90 sec | Return one step |

Goal: Reach the upper number only if every repetition or second is controlled. Repeat Week 4 as needed; finishing it does not require choosing the hardest variations.

## Progression rules

Progressive overload means gradually making training more challenging as your ability grows.

After any session, progress an exercise only when all of these are true:

- You completed every set at the top of the listed range.
- Technique stayed controlled from first repetition to last.
- You had about 2–3 good repetitions left at the end of each set.
- You had no concerning pain during the exercise and recovered normally before the next session.

At the next session, change only one thing:

1. First add 1 repetition per set or 5 seconds per hold, staying within the week’s range.
2. Once you own the top of the range for two sessions, make the exercise one small step harder: use a slightly lower push-up incline, squat a little deeper, use a slightly stronger band, or reduce pull-up assistance.
3. Return to the bottom of the repetition range after making the exercise harder.

If you cannot reach the minimum range or your form worsens, make the variation easier. This is adjustment, not failure.

## Five-minute cooldown

A cooldown is an easy transition from training back to rest.

1. Walk slowly or march gently — 2 minutes.
2. Take 4–6 slow breaths, allowing your breathing rate to settle.
3. Optional gentle stretches — 15–30 seconds each for chest, front of thighs, buttocks, and back. Move into mild tension, never pain; do not bounce.

Stretching should feel comfortable. It does not need to be intense to “count.”

## Recovery and consistency

- Leave at least one day between these full-body sessions at first.
- Mild muscle soreness can happen, especially after a new exercise. If soreness changes how you move, use an easier session, walk gently, or take another rest day.
- Persistent, severe, swollen, or worsening pain is not a progression signal; stop the provoking movement and seek qualified advice.
- Eat regular balanced meals and drink according to thirst. No supplement is required to begin this plan.
- Keep a repeatable appointment: same days, approximate time, and prepared equipment.
- If you miss a session, resume with the next planned session. Do not double the workout.
- If you miss a full week, repeat the last comfortable week or reduce each exercise by one set.
- A 15-minute shortened session is better than skipping: warm up, then do one or two sets each of squat, push, row, bridge, and plank.

## Track progress in under two minutes

Record this after each workout:

| Date | Exercise and version | Sets × reps/time | Effort: reps left | Pain or form notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example | Counter push-up | 2 × 8 | About 3 | Smooth; try 9 next time |

Also note one consistency win each week, such as “completed all three sessions” or “used an easier option instead of forcing poor form.” Progress includes better control, greater range, less assistance, more repetitions, longer holds, and simply showing up.

## Common beginner mistakes

- Starting with the hardest version: Choose the version you control, not the one that looks most impressive.
- Doing too much too soon: Follow the listed sets. Add only one variable at a time.
- Rushing repetitions: Use roughly 2 seconds down and 1–2 seconds up; pause if momentum takes over.
- Chasing exhaustion: Finish with about 2 good repetitions in reserve.
- Ignoring pulling work: Rows and assisted-pull work balance the pushing in the plan.
- Holding the breath: Exhale during the hard part and inhale during the easier return.
- Copying someone else’s range: Use the pain-free range you can control.
- Using unsafe furniture or anchors: Stability and load rating matter more than convenience.
- Changing the plan every session: Repeat movements long enough to learn them and see progress.
- Treating one missed day as failure: Continue at the next opportunity; consistency is built over months, not judged by one week.

## After Week 4

Repeat Week 4 for another 2–4 weeks, progressing one movement at a time. When you can perform the top range comfortably for two sessions, choose a slightly harder variation and return to the lower end of the range.

A useful long-term routine can remain this simple. ACSM’s evidence review emphasizes that bodyweight training is effective, advanced methods are often unnecessary for general fitness, and regular participation matters more than complexity.[1]
